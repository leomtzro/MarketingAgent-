Takeaway: start in a deliberately small group so a few people quickly feel the service is theirs. Widen after it works.

## Graham's pattern
- Facebook began only at Harvard, then at a few colleges. Zuckerberg told Graham that building course lists school by school made students feel the site was theirs.
- Marketplaces need a subset of the market first. Ask which subset would give critical mass fastest.
- Many founders do it without noticing: they build for themselves and friends, who are early adopters. Founders who lose that edge (friends not early adopters) must find them deliberately.
- Find early users by watching who is most enthusiastic after a loose launch, then seek more like them (Pinterest and design bloggers).

## For us
1. Today: [[Lead generation plan]] says whichever clinic type answers first, and narrow to one after about 20 conversations. This note is the rule for narrowing.
2. Pick the niche using three tests: they answer fast, they share the same pain in their own words, and owners know each other (word of mouth in one Tijuana area or one specialty).
3. Candidates to test, not decided: dermatology and aesthetic clinics (client zero is a dermatologist), dental, orthopedics. Order and size of each market in Tijuana: `unknown`.
4. Talk to that group in their language: [[Narrowcasting]], [[Second-slide customers]], [[Customer avatar]].
5. Case study from clinic 1 goes first to people in the same niche.

## Why it holds elsewhere (shared rule)
Concentrated effort reaches a threshold that spreading thin never reaches: a small fire is hot enough to light bigger logs; a band that fills one local bar is talked about more than one that plays ten empty rooms.

## Limits
Graham's examples are consumer networks and marketplaces; clinic owners may not talk to each other. How much they do is `unknown`; ask in the first conversations.
